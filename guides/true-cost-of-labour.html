The true cost of labour: a guide for UK builders

If you pay someone £20 an hour, an hour of their work costs your business more than £20. The same goes for your own time if you work alone. This guide explains where the extra comes from and how to work out your own figure, so it ends up in your price instead of coming out of your profit.

Hourly pay isn't the cost of an hour

Two things push the real cost up:

  • Extra costs on top of pay. Some costs only exist because someone works for you, such as employer contributions, protective gear and training.
  • Hours you pay for but can't charge for. Holidays, travel between jobs, collecting materials and rained-off days are all paid time that no customer pays for directly.

Your prices have to cover both. The hours you can charge for have to pay for the hours you can't.

What adds to the cost of labour

Not all of these will apply to you, and the amounts vary a lot from one business to the next. Use the list as a checklist, not a set of standard figures.

  • Employer costs. If you employ staff, you may pay employer's National Insurance and workplace pension contributions on top of their wages. The rules, rates and thresholds change, so take your figures from your payroll records or your accountant.
  • Holiday pay. Paid holiday and bank holidays are weeks you pay for when nobody is on site.
  • Sickness, training and other paid time off. Any paid time away from jobs works the same way as holiday.
  • Insurance. Some insurance costs go up with the number of people you employ. Count the share that belongs to your workers.
  • PPE, workwear and training. Boots, hi-vis, gloves, eye and ear protection, and courses and cards your workers need.
  • Travel time. Paid time spent driving to and between jobs, or to the merchant.
  • Non-productive time. Waiting for deliveries, bad weather, tidying the yard, returning to fix snags, and time between jobs.

Running costs such as the van, fuel, phone and accountant are better counted as overhead. There's more on that below.

Paid hours vs chargeable hours

Start from the hours you pay for in a year, take off the weeks nobody is working, then take off the time at work that you can't put on a customer's job. What's left are your chargeable hours. They are the hours that have to cover the whole year's labour cost.

True cost per hour = Total yearly labour cost ÷ Chargeable hours

Worked example

All the numbers below are made up to show the method. They aren't typical figures, statutory rates or a recommendation. Use your own.

A builder employs one worker on £20 an hour, 40 hours a week, paid for all 52 weeks of the year including holidays.

£47,900 ÷ 1,564 hours = £30.63 per chargeable hour

So in this example, each hour the worker spends on a customer's job really costs about £30.63, not £20. That's around 53% more than the hourly rate. If the builder priced labour at £20 an hour, the difference would come out of the profit on every job.

Putting it into the calculator

The ContractorQuote calculator asks for the hourly rate and a labour burden percentage, which is the extra cost on top of that rate. You can turn your yearly figures into a burden percentage like this:

Labour burden % = (Total yearly labour cost ÷ (Chargeable hours × Hourly rate) − 1) × 100

For the example: £47,900 ÷ (1,564 × £20) = 1.53, so the labour burden is about 53%.

For a job the builder expects to take 40 hours, entering 40 hours at £20 with a 53% labour burden gives:

Example labour cost for a 40-hour job
Labour: 40 hours × £20£800.00
Labour burden: 53%£424.00
Labour cost for the job£1,224.00

That's close to 40 × £30.63, as it should be. Enter only the hours you expect to spend on the job itself. The time between jobs is already covered by the burden percentage.

If you work on your own

If you're self-employed with no staff, you won't have employer contributions, but the rest still applies. Nobody pays you for holidays, sick days, quoting, paperwork or driving to the merchant. Use the same method: decide what you need to earn in a year, add your own labour-related costs such as PPE and training, and divide by the hours you can realistically charge for.

Labour cost or overhead? Count each cost once

Some costs could go in either place. The van is a good example: you could treat it as a labour cost or as overhead. It doesn't matter much which you choose, as long as each cost is counted once.

A simple rule is to put costs that go up and down with the number of people working, such as wages, employer contributions and PPE, into the labour burden. Put the costs of running the business, such as the van, fuel, phone, software and accountant, into overhead. The pricing guide explains how to work out an overhead percentage.

Check your figures

  • Use last year's real figures where you have them: payroll records, invoices and your diary.
  • Recheck at least once a year, and whenever wages or your team change.
  • For employer contributions, holiday rules and pensions, ask your accountant or check GOV.UK's guidance on employing people.
